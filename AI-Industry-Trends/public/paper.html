ABSTRACT

Different industries are adopting AI at different speeds, so it's hard to tell which ones are falling behind — and knowing that helps businesses and policymakers know where to put support or investment.

This project predicts how close each industry is to reaching mainstream (50%) AI adoption, using the Global AI Adoption Trends by Industry Sector, Country, and Business Function dataset[1], which covers adoption rates across 15 industries from 2022–2025. After engineering features like growth rate and how fast that growth is changing, three regression models — Linear Regression, Random Forest, and a Neural Network — were trained to predict each industry's remaining gap to 50% adoption.

Random Forest predicted the adoption gap with about 99% accuracy and an average error under one percentage point — close to exact. That suggests adoption gaps can be predicted fairly reliably from historical trend data, which could help identify which industries are lagging and where attention is needed to help them catch up.

01 — WHY THIS MATTERS

1Introduction

AI has gone from a niche, futuristic technology to a normal part of how businesses run — but not every industry is picking it up at the same speed. A national survey of U.S. firms found adoption intensity varies widely by sector: healthcare and professional services show relatively high usage, while construction and retail lag behind at only around 4% adoption[2]. That unevenness makes it hard for businesses, investors, and policymakers to know which industries are falling behind and which are almost caught up.

This project asks: by analyzing how fast an industry has adopted AI in the past, can we predict how close it is to reaching mainstream (50%) adoption? A recent analysis of global AI tool adoption from 2023–2025 confirms some industries are racing ahead while others risk falling further behind, using trend-based projections — though without pinpointing a specific numerical distance left to close for each industry[3]. A separate study across countries and industries similarly found that the pace of adoption depends heavily on which industry is being examined, reinforcing that a one-size-fits-all measure doesn't capture the real picture[4]. Predicting that gap directly gives a clearer, more precise idea of where support is actually needed.

This is a supervised regression problem: the target is a number, not a category. The data is entirely numerical — yearly adoption rates plus engineered features like year-over-year growth, how fast that growth is speeding up or slowing down, and a normalized time value — across 15 industries from 2022 to 2025. The prediction target, gap_to_50, is the distance between an industry's current adoption and the 50% mainstream mark. Three models spanning simple to complex — Linear Regression, Random Forest, and a Neural Network — let us compare a basic baseline against a tree-based method and a more flexible model, to see which actually explains the data best.

02 — PRIOR WORK

2Background

Most existing work on AI adoption describes what's happening rather than predicting what happens next. One NBER study asked companies directly about their AI use and found healthcare and professional services far ahead of construction and retail (~4%) — but it captured only a single point in time, with no year-over-year tracking[2]. Another study tracked AI tool adoption globally from 2023–2025, projecting the current year forward to estimate future adoption — similar in spirit to the time-based features used here, but focused on specific tools and factors like age and location rather than whole industries reaching a mainstream threshold[3]. A third study, using five countries and ten industries, found the relationship between AI adoption and job loss differed sharply by industry, especially retail[4] — supporting the choice to treat industry sector as its own feature rather than pooling everything together.

03 — 75 RECORDS, 15 SECTORS, 2022–2025

3Dataset

75 records span 15 industry sectors — Agriculture, Education, Energy & Materials, Environment/Climate, Financial Services/BFSI, Government & Public, Healthcare & Pharma, Legal Services, Logistics & Supply Chain, Manufacturing, Media & Telecom, Professional Services, Real Estate & Construction, Retail & Consumer Goods, and Technology/Software. Columns include Year, Adoption Rate, and engineered time features: delta_1 (change from the prior period), yoy_growth, accel (growth-rate change), time_idx (a normalized time index), and the prediction target gap_to_50. The dataset also includes cross-sector influence scores for related domains like HR, Knowledge Management, Marketing & Sales, and Risk & Finance. Industry sector was label-encoded, and the data split 80/20 into train and test sets.

Figure 2 in the underlying analysis tracked this same progression year over year: most sectors show a sharp jump in year-over-year growth around 2023, then level off approaching 2025, with Technology/Software consistently growing fastest and Agriculture and Environment/Climate trailing throughout.

Engineered features

time_idx(t) = the ordinal position of year t within a sector's sequence (0, 1, 2, 3, 4)
gap_to_50(t) = 0.50 − A(t)

These capture not just where a sector stands, but how quickly it has been moving and whether that speed is increasing or leveling off. time_idx gives the models a clean, normalized way to represent time; gap_to_50 is the prediction target itself, calculated directly as 0.50 minus each sector's adoption rate.

04 — THREE MODELS, SIMPLE TO FLEXIBLE

4Methodology

4.1 — Linear Regression

Used first as a simple baseline: it fits a straight line through the data that best matches the relationship between input features and the target, with no hyperparameters to tune.

4.2 — Random Forest Regression

A stronger, tree-based model: it builds a large group of decision trees, each trained on a random slice of the data, and averages their predictions — usually better at picking up nonlinear patterns and more resistant to overfitting than a single tree. A hyperparameter sweep over max_depth from 1 to 9 found that MAE dropped sharply between depth 1 and depth 2, then leveled off from depth 3 onward — a shallow tree was enough for strong performance.

4.3 — Neural Network

Used to test whether a more flexible model could capture even more complex patterns: a small multi-layer perceptron with three hidden layers, trained for up to 1,000 iterations.

Random Forest performed strongly — R² of 0.99, MAE of about 0.014 in cross-sector evaluation — suggesting the engineered features carry real predictive signal. Linear Regression's perfect R² of 1.0 is the flag, not the win.

Per-sector view: Linear Regression's errors sit at the 1e-15 scale across every industry — the clearest sign of leakage rather than a genuinely perfect model. Random Forest's errors stay small and consistent, from near 0 up to about 0.02 MAE, with Government & Public and Financial Services/BFSI running slightly higher than the rest. The Neural Network's errors are far larger and uneven, from about 0.1 to 0.4 MAE, worst in Education and Energy & Materials, best in Environment/Climate.

5.1 — Discussion and limitations

The most unexpected result — Linear Regression's perfect R² — is not realistic for a problem like this, and points to data leakage: Adoption Rate was most likely still present in the input features alongside the target gap_to_50, which is calculated directly from it. The Neural Network's negative R² is most likely explained by unscaled input features, since neural networks are sensitive to input range, and a convergence warning during training suggests it never settled into a good solution.

A key limitation is the small sample size — only 75 records — which makes any single train/test split harder to fully trust and raises the risk of overfitting to patterns that don't generalize. Next steps include scaling inputs before retraining the Neural Network, and using cross-validation to get a more reliable performance estimate given the limited data.

06 — TAKEAWAY

6Conclusion

Of the three models tested, Random Forest performed best — R² of 0.99, average error of about 0.014 — showing that engineered features like growth rate and acceleration carry real information about where an industry is headed. Linear Regression's suspicious R² of 1.0 points to a data leakage problem that needs fixing before it can be fairly compared to the other two. The Neural Network underperformed, likely in part due to features not being scaled before training.

Overall, adoption gaps can be predicted fairly accurately from past adoption data — which could help businesses and policymakers decide which industries need the most support to catch up. Next steps: fix the Linear Regression leakage, scale features and retrain the Neural Network, add cross-validation, switch to a time-based train/test split given this is time-series data, and look more closely at which sectors the models tend to over- or under-predict.
